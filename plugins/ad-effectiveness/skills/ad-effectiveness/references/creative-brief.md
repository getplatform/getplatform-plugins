# Creative Brief Template

A brief that produces effective creative has one objective, one insight, and one emotional territory. Every additional objective dilutes the creative focus. If the brief cannot be summarized in two sentences, it is not ready.

---

## Brief Template

### 1. Background / Context

State the business problem the creative must solve. Not the marketing problem — the business problem.

- What is changing in the market, the audience, or the category?
- Why does this campaign need to exist now?
- What has been tried before, and why is that not sufficient?

**Weak:** "We need more brand awareness."
**Strong:** "We are losing consideration at the category entry point because buyers default to incumbents without evaluating us. We need to build positive associations before they enter the market, not after."

---

### 2. Target Audience

Name a specific segment, not a demographic bracket. The more specific and human the description, the better the brief.

Include:
- Who they are in their own terms (role, situation, mindset)
- What they care about that is relevant to the campaign
- What they believe before seeing this ad
- What we need them to feel after seeing it

**Weak:** "Marketing managers, 28–45, at mid-market B2B SaaS companies."
**Strong:** "Marketing managers at mid-market SaaS companies who are privately unsure whether their current ad approach is working — but unwilling to admit it to their leadership. They trust peer recommendations over brand claims. They scroll past ads that feel like ads."

---

### 3. Single Human Truth

The one insight that makes the creative feel earned and real. This is not a product truth — it is a human truth that exists before the brand enters the picture.

Test: does this insight make someone say "that's exactly it"? If it makes them say "yes, I suppose that's true," it is not sharp enough.

**Weak:** "People want tools that save them time."
**Strong:** "When your ad campaign looks the same as last year's, you feel it before you see it in the numbers — but you run it anyway because changing it feels riskier than staying put."

The human truth is the bridge between the audience's lived experience and the emotional territory.

---

### 4. Emotional Territory

Name the primary emotion the creative should trigger, and a secondary emotion that adds texture. Be specific — "positive" is not an emotion.

**Primary emotions that build long-term brand equity:**
- Warmth and belonging (the feeling of being understood, of not being alone)
- Joy and delight (unexpected happiness, playfulness)
- Inspiration and ambition (the feeling that something is possible that wasn't before)
- Nostalgia and longing (bittersweet connection to something true and past)
- Amusement (wry recognition, shared absurdity)

**Examples of emotional territory in practice:**

| Territory | Primary | Secondary | Real example |
|-----------|---------|-----------|-------------|
| "The warmth of expertise" | Warmth | Quiet confidence | John Lewis Christmas ads |
| "Shared absurdity" | Amusement | Surprise | Compare the Market meerkats |
| "You can do this" | Inspiration | Warmth | Nike "Find Your Greatness" |
| "We see you" | Belonging | Relief | Dove Real Beauty |

Do not use:
- "Be funny" (not an emotional territory — a tonal instruction)
- "Exciting" (not an emotion)
- "Premium" (not an emotion — a brand attribute)

---

### 5. Role of the Brand

How does the brand fit into the human truth? What does the brand make possible that was not possible before?

The brand should be the enabler of the resolution — not the interruption, not the hero who saves the day from outside, but the natural context in which resolution becomes possible.

**Weak:** "Brand X helps marketing managers work smarter."
**Strong:** "Brand X is the confidence that you've already seen this problem clearly, before anyone else has."

---

### 6. Distinctive Asset Requirements

List the brand's distinctive assets (DBAs) and specify which must appear and how.

DBAs are brand elements that have been built up over time and are strongly associated with the brand in memory. They are not logos — they are any sensory or structural element that signals the brand before conscious recognition.

Common DBAs to list:
- Color palette (primary and secondary)
- Typography or headline style
- Brand character or spokesperson
- Sound signature or music style
- Tagline or verbal register
- Visual composition style (close-up, wide shot, texture)

**In the brief, specify:**
- Which DBAs must appear (mandatory)
- Which DBAs should appear if possible (recommended)
- How they should be integrated (naturally in the story, not appended)

---

### 7. Mandatories and Non-Negotiables

List hard constraints: legal, regulatory, brand, partner.

- Required claims or disclaimers
- Forbidden language or imagery
- Logo lockup requirements
- Product accuracy requirements
- Partner or licensor restrictions

Keep this list short. If it is longer than five items, the brief is overloaded.

---

### 8. Executional Guidance

What to avoid. What tone is off-limits. What has been tried and failed. This section prevents the agency from wasting time on directions you already know are wrong.

Examples:
- "Do not open on a problem — we have tested fear-led executions and they underperform consistently."
- "Avoid stock footage of laptops and people smiling at screens."
- "Tone should feel human and slightly irreverent — not corporate, not startup-breathless."
- "Do not use the word 'seamless.'"

---

### 9. Success Criteria

Specify how the creative will be evaluated before media spend is committed. This removes ambiguity from the approval process and prevents subjective preferences from derailing effective creative.

**Pre-spend evaluation:**
- Does it score 3+ stars on System1 or equivalent emotional response test?
- Can five external viewers name the emotion and the brand without being told the brief?
- Does it pass the 5-question evaluation rubric? (See `evaluation-rubric.md`)

**Post-campaign evaluation:**
- Brand tracking uplift (awareness, consideration, preference) over 6–12 months
- Mental availability metrics if available
- Sales and revenue contribution over 18-month window (not just campaign period)

## Anti-Brief: What Makes Briefs Fail

Most briefs that produce bad creative are bad in one of five ways.

### 1. Multiple objectives

"We want to build brand awareness, drive trial sign-ups, and educate the market about our new features."

A brief with three objectives produces creative that does none of them well. Force a single objective before briefing.

### 2. No real human insight

"Our audience is busy professionals who want efficiency."

This is a demographic observation, not an insight. Real insights are uncomfortable, specific, and surprising. They require talking to real people, not describing a target.

### 3. Emotional territory is a tone instruction

"We want it to feel premium and innovative."

Premium and innovative describe a brand attribute, not an emotion. The viewer cannot feel "premium" — they can feel pride, envy, aspiration, or exclusivity. Name the actual emotion.

### 4. Too many mandatories

If the creative has fifteen required elements — six product features, three brand messages, two partner logos, a disclaimer, and a CTA — it cannot tell a story. Mandatories should fit on three lines.

### 5. "Be creative" with no direction

Leaving emotional territory, human truth, and brand role blank and asking the agency to "interpret the brand freely" produces beautiful work that does not build brand equity for anyone in particular.

---

## Brief Quality Check

Before sending the brief to any creative team, confirm:

- [ ] One clear business objective (not a list)
- [ ] Audience described in human terms, not demographics
- [ ] Single human truth that makes someone say "that's exactly it"
- [ ] Named primary emotion (not "positive" or "exciting")
- [ ] Brand role is clear (enabler, not interruption)
- [ ] Mandatory DBA list is specific and short
- [ ] Mandatories list is under five items
- [ ] Success criteria are measurable and pre-agreed

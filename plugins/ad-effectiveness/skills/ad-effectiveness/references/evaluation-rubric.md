# Ad Evaluation Rubric

Use this rubric before approving creative for production spend, and when giving feedback to creative teams. Ground all feedback in observable creative decisions, not subjective preferences.

---

## System1's 1-5 Star Rating: Expanded Criteria

### 1 Star — Flat

**What it means:** No measurable emotional response. The ad generates nothing — no positive feeling, no negative feeling, no surprise. The viewer is not changed by having seen it.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- Screen recording of software with voiceover listing features
- Stock footage of diverse professionals in bright offices
- Explainer animation with feature bullets and a logo
- "We help [audience] do [task] faster and smarter." Followed by a CTA.

**When to run it:** Never for brand advertising. Acceptable only as a last resort retargeting unit for audiences who are already in the purchase decision, where the offer itself does all the work.

---

### 2 Stars — Weak Signal

**What it means:** Some mild positive feeling — a flicker of warmth, a brief smile, mild interest — but insufficient to create memory. Brand linkage is low. The ad is pleasant and forgotten.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- A customer testimonial with genuine warmth but no story arc
- A product demo with a relatable scenario but no tension or resolution
- A well-shot lifestyle ad with attractive imagery and no narrative

**When to run it:** Acceptable for direct response to warm audiences. For brand campaigns, insufficient. Rebrief for stronger Fame.

---

### 3 Stars — Good

**What it means:** Clear positive emotional response. The viewer feels something real — warmth, amusement, inspiration. The brand lands naturally. The ad would be recognized by target viewers as belonging to the brand.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- A short story with a relatable setup, a moment of tension, and a warm resolution
- Brand appears naturally in the resolution, not bolted onto the ending
- Distinctive brand assets are present without being forced
- Viewer could describe the emotional experience after one viewing

**When to run it:** This is the minimum bar for brand advertising. Approve and test media behind it.

---

### 4 Stars — Excellent

**What it means:** Strong emotional response with clear brand linkage. Viewers remember both the story and the brand. Highly shareable. Likely to generate earned media. Builds strong associations.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- A story that viewers retell to others without being asked
- Brand appears at the emotional peak, not just the end
- Distinctive assets are woven into the story itself
- Multiple viewings increase enjoyment rather than decrease it

**When to run it:** Commit significant media budget. Protect this creative from being changed prematurely.

---

### 5 Stars — Exceptional (Fluent Device territory)

**What it means:** The creative introduces or extends a world, character, or tonal signature with which viewers develop an ongoing relationship. Each future execution inherits the accumulated warmth.

**What it looks like in practice:**
- A character or world that viewers actively anticipate
- Executions that reward prior familiarity (the returning viewer gets more than the new one)
- Emotional response increases over time as the relationship deepens
- Brand is inseparable from the creative world — you cannot imagine one without the other

**When to run it:** Protect this creative system vigorously. Do not reset it for novelty. Produce further executions within the world.

## Pre-Spend Evaluation Checklist

Apply these five questions to any piece of creative before approving production or media spend. Score each as Pass, Weak, or Fail.

### Question 1: Does it make you feel something?

Watch the ad once, without pausing. Immediately after, name the primary emotion. If you cannot name a specific emotion — or if the emotion is "fine" or "okay" — score it Fail for brand purposes.

- **Pass**: "I felt warm / amused / inspired / nostalgic"
- **Weak**: "It seemed positive / pleasant / competent"
- **Fail**: "It was clear / informative / professional"

### Question 2: Is the feeling positive?

Name the primary emotion. Assess whether it is one that builds positive brand associations over time.

- **Pass**: Joy, warmth, amusement, inspiration, nostalgia, delight, surprise (positive)
- **Weak**: Mild curiosity, mild intrigue (insufficient for brand building)
- **Fail**: Fear, anxiety, urgency, contempt, guilt (damages long-term brand equity)

Note: Fear and urgency are not always wrong. They are appropriate for direct response to in-market buyers. They are damaging for brand advertising to cold audiences.

### Question 3: Would you know it was ours without the logo?

Mentally remove the brand name and logo from every frame. Does the tonal signature, color, character, or world still signal your brand?

- **Pass**: Yes — the voice, world, or visual style is unmistakably ours
- **Weak**: Possibly — if you know the brand, you might guess it
- **Fail**: No — this could be any brand in the category

### Question 4: Does it use our distinctive assets?

List the brand's DBAs. Check each one:

- Does it appear in the creative?
- Does it appear naturally in the story, or is it forced onto the end frame?
- Does it appear often enough to build or reinforce memory?

- **Pass**: Core DBAs present, integrated naturally
- **Weak**: DBAs present only in the final logo lockup
- **Fail**: No DBAs present; creative could belong to anyone

### Question 5: Does it fit into a long-term creative world?

Could you produce three more executions in the same world without starting over?

- **Pass**: Yes — the world, character, or tonal register is extensible
- **Weak**: Possibly — with significant additional creative development
- **Fail**: No — this is a one-off with no creative system behind it

## Emotion Mapping: Ekman's 7 Basic Emotions

Paul Ekman identified seven basic emotions expressed universally across cultures. Use this map when identifying what an ad is actually doing and whether it serves the campaign objective.

| Emotion | Build long-term brand equity? | Use for brand advertising? | Use for direct response? |
|---------|------------------------------|---------------------------|-------------------------|
| **Joy** | Yes — strongly | Yes | Rarely needed |
| **Surprise** | Yes — when positive | Yes (positive surprise) | Sometimes |
| **Disgust** | No | No | Rarely, for contrast |
| **Anger** | No | No | Sometimes (injustice framing) |
| **Fear** | No | No | Yes, carefully |
| **Sadness** | Sometimes (bittersweet) | Rarely, with expertise | No |
| **Contempt** | No | No | No |

**Practical notes:**
- Warmth, amusement, and nostalgia are derived positive emotions — not on Ekman's basic list but consistently high-performers in brand advertising
- Sadness can work in brand advertising when it resolves into hope or warmth (John Lewis, Procter & Gamble). It fails when it ends in sadness without resolution.
- Contempt toward competitors is a specific failure mode — it makes the viewer feel superior but associates the brand with negativity

## Common Creative Failure Modes

### Failure Mode 1: The Demo Reel

**What it is:** A product walkthrough presented as an ad. Features listed in sequence. No story. No human.

**Why it fails:** It communicates what the product does, not what it means. Features are forgotten immediately. Stories persist. A viewer cannot retell a feature list, but they can retell a story.

**The fix:** Start with the human experience before the product. What does it feel like to have the problem this product solves? Build story first, product second.

---

### Failure Mode 2: Logo Soup

**What it is:** Multiple brand logos, partner logos, or client logos competing for attention in a single frame or spot.

**Why it fails:** Every additional logo dilutes the brand linkage for each individual brand. Fluency requires one brand to be dominant. Logo soup produces zero fluency for all brands present.

**The fix:** One brand per ad. Partner logos in print/static only, at low visual weight.

---

### Failure Mode 3: Late Brand Reveal

**What it is:** Brand appears only in the final two seconds of a 30-second spot. The story builds emotional response and then the brand takes credit at the last moment.

**Why it fails:** Memory encoding links the emotion to whatever was present when the emotion peaked — not to whatever appeared afterward. If the brand appears after the emotional peak, the viewer associates the feeling with the story, not the brand. Fluency is broken.

**The fix:** Weave the brand into the story at the point of resolution. The brand should be present when the emotion is highest, not after.

---

### Failure Mode 4: Generic Positivity

**What it is:** Happy, diverse, photogenic people using a product and smiling. No story arc. No tension. No resolution. Just sustained brightness.

**Why it fails:** Sustained positivity without story creates no emotional event — and therefore no memory. The brain encodes change and surprise, not flatness. Happy people using a product is visual wallpaper.

**The fix:** Introduce a small tension. Even minor friction — a moment of doubt, a challenge, a decision — gives the resolution its emotional power.

---

### Failure Mode 5: Fear and Urgency Overuse

**What it is:** Every ad leading with a problem, a risk, a deadline, or a consequence. "Are you leaving money on the table?" "Your competitors are already doing this." "Act now before it's too late."

**Why it fails:** In isolation, urgency works. As the dominant creative register, it trains the audience to associate the brand with threat. Over time, brand recall becomes negative affect. The audience tunes out or actively avoids the brand.

**The fix:** Reserve urgency and fear for direct response to in-market audiences. Replace it with aspiration, warmth, or humor for brand campaigns to cold audiences.

## Giving Feedback Using the 3F Framework

Structure all creative feedback using Fame, Feeling, and Fluency. This removes subjective preference from the conversation and focuses the team on what the creative is actually doing.

**Feedback structure:**

1. **Observe what the creative is doing** (not what you feel about it)
2. **Map it to Fame, Feeling, or Fluency**
3. **State the specific change needed and why**

**Example feedback using 3F:**

Weak: "I don't really like the tone. It feels a bit flat to me."

Strong: "On Fame — I watched this twice and could not identify a clear emotional peak. There's no moment where I felt something. On Feeling — the closest emotion I could name was mild satisfaction, but it dissipated immediately. The fix is to introduce a moment of tension between seconds 8 and 14 that makes the resolution in the final third feel earned. Without the tension, there is no emotional event for the brain to encode."

## The Endline Test

The endline (final frame, final line, final image) is where the emotional residue of the ad is deposited. Apply this test to every execution.

**The question:** Does the last frame create a feeling, or does it state a fact?

- **Feeling**: "For the nights that matter." — leaves the viewer in an emotional state
- **Fact**: "Start your free 14-day trial today." — leaves the viewer with a task

For brand advertising, end on feeling. The CTA is for the landing page, not the film.

**Test it:** Cover the logo in the final frame. Read or listen to the last line. Does it produce an emotional response? Or does it close the door?

If it closes the door, rewrite the endline. The ad should end by opening something — a feeling, a possibility, a question — not by demanding an action.
